\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Compulsive safety behaviour is commonly explained by biased beliefs about danger
or control, or by impaired learning. This article identifies a complementary
mechanism that requires neither deficit. In a hazard model, an episode starts in danger with
probability $q$, and each preventive repetition succeeds with probability $p$.
If an agent always repeats the action $k$ times, outcomes reveal only
$\lamk = q(1-p)^k$. The Fisher information is rank one, so $q$ and $p$ are
\emph{structurally non-identifiable} under any fixed-$k$ policy, regardless of
sample size. This theorem does not depend on a biased prior. Repetition removes
the variation needed to separate the two causes.
Simulations of exact Bayesian learning show why this geometry matters: with an
initially pessimistic prior, a repetitive agent becomes precise about the observed
harm probability while remaining wrong about its causes; varying $k$ recovers both
parameters. Interventions restore information only when they create contrasts
between action counts. Uncertainty aversion increases repetition and worsens
causal learning, but it does not sustain repetition over the simulated horizon.
The model therefore explains why causal beliefs can remain wrong; it does not, by
itself, explain why repetitive behaviour persists. It predicts a direct human
test: schedules matched on mean action count but differing in where they vary
should produce different learning about personal efficacy, with expected
information outperforming raw behavioural variance. The result is a falsifiable
mechanism for failed causal learning, not a complete account of
obsessive-compulsive disorder.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Author Summary}

People who repeatedly perform a safety action can learn that the immediate outcome is safe
without learning whether safety came from the world being safe or from their action. In this study, I
formalise that problem by modelling a person who faces danger with probability $q$ and whose preventive
action sometimes works. If the person always repeats the action the same number of times, the observations
contain only one combined measure of danger and action success. No amount of experience can
separate the two causes. With Bayesian simulations, I show that an ideally rational learner can
therefore become confident about what will happen while remaining uncertain, or becoming confidently wrong, about why.
Small, well-chosen changes in the number of repetitions restore the missing information. The model makes a
direct experimental prediction: groups assigned the same average number of safety actions should learn
differently when those actions are distributed across more informative repetition counts. The account
explains why a person's belief about efficacy can remain wrong. It does not, by itself, explain why the
person continues to repeat the behaviour. Its contribution is narrower: protection can prevent the
comparisons needed for causal learning even when the learner updates evidence correctly.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} obsessive-compulsive disorder; structural
identifiability; Bayesian learning; safety behaviour; exposure and response
prevention.

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Safe outcomes can conceal their cause}

A safety action can prevent harm while making its own efficacy difficult to
learn. Consider a person who performs the same preventive action several times
whenever danger is possible. A safe outcome is compatible with two explanations:
the danger was unlikely to begin with, or the action removed it. If the number of
actions never changes, experience does not provide the comparison needed to
separate those explanations. The person may learn to predict the outcome while
remaining wrong about why it occurred.

This article asks whether that failure can arise without biased inference or an
impaired learning rule. The target is deliberately narrow. The model concerns
knowledge of two latent causes, baseline hazard and preventive efficacy. It does
not attempt to reproduce the full phenomenology of compulsions; it isolates
repeated preventive actions that alter a latent hazard. The following sections
show that the missing comparison produces a structural identification problem.

\subsection{Existing explanations and an information-structural account}

Two computational accounts of compulsion are well developed.
\citet{rigoux2024beliefs} model a handwashing scenario as a partially observable
Markov decision process and show that a single belief distortion --- an
underestimation of the probability that one's preventive action succeeds ---
triggers and maintains compulsive behaviour and scales with its severity. On this
account the pathology sits in a \emph{belief}: the agent's point estimate of its
own efficacy is too low. \citet{sakai2022eligibility} locate the pathology
instead in a \emph{learning mechanism}: an imbalance between the memory traces
that assign credit to reinforcement and to punishment. Related accounts place it
in uncertainty about state transitions
\citep{fradkin2020searching,fradkin2020latent,prudon2021,sharp2023transition}, in
habitual avoidance \citep{gillan2014enhanced}, or in a failure to integrate
local evidence into a stable global belief. In the
clinical literature the corresponding idea is that safety behaviour preserves
threat beliefs by preventing their disconfirmation
\citep{lovibond2009safety,craske2014inhibitory}, and that low-cost avoidance
survives even after fear has been extinguished \citep{vervliet2015low}.

This article develops a third possibility. Even with exact belief updating and
credit assignment, an agent can remain ignorant of its own efficacy because its
policy determines which comparisons enter the data, and a fixed policy supplies
none. The mechanism lies in the evidence generated by behaviour rather than in a
failure to update that evidence.

\citet{rigoux2024beliefs} state a related gap: their model does not describe how
beliefs about world contingencies are acquired, and its agent parameters are
static point estimates rather than time-varying posteriors. The extension here
makes that acquisition problem explicit and separates consequences of the
information structure from consequences of belief or credit-assignment parameters.

\subsection{Contributions and scope}

\paragraph{A mathematical result.} Under a fixed repetition count, baseline
hazard and personal efficacy are non-identifiable, not merely difficult to
estimate. The terminal-outcome information matrix is rank one for every $k$ and
every interior $(q,p)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rank1}).

\paragraph{A mechanism independent of learner failure.} The result holds for an
ideal Bayesian learner, so it does not require impaired updating or credit
assignment. The simulations use a deliberately pessimistic initial prior; the
theorem itself is prior-independent. The claim is about the evidence generated
by a policy, not about how people with obsessive-compulsive disorder learn in
general.

\paragraph{A strict behavioural boundary.} The simulations separate persistence
of a causal error from persistence of the action. Uncertainty aversion increases
repetition and worsens learning, but repetition still declines over the simulated
horizon. The model explains why causal beliefs can remain wrong; it does not, by
itself, explain why repetitive behaviour persists.

\paragraph{A sharper account of response prevention.} Setting $k=0$ samples the
baseline hazard $q$ but supplies zero information about $p$. Response prevention
is informative about efficacy only when it is combined with episodes at a
different action count. What restores identifiability is policy variation, not
the absence of safety behaviour by itself.

\paragraph{A direct experimental test.} A randomized experiment can hold the
mean number of actions fixed while varying their distribution across trials. The
model predicts that efficacy learning will follow the expected information of the
schedule rather than its mean or raw variance alone.

\subsection{The general principle}

Knowing whether an action controls an outcome requires variation in the action
and comparison of the resulting outcomes. When failure is costly, the policy that
minimises immediate risk can suppress exactly that variation. The same structure
appears as a failure of persistent excitation in system identification, as
self-confirming equilibrium in economics \citep{fudenberg1993selfconfirming}, and
as selective labels in machine learning \citep{lakkaraju2017selective}. The
clinical application motivates the model; the identification problem is more
general.

\section{Model}

\subsection{Relation to the point-belief model}
\label{sec:baseline}

The analysis is motivated by, but does not reproduce, the full partially
observable Markov decision process of \citet{rigoux2024beliefs}. In that model,
objective world parameters generate state transitions and observations, whereas
subjective point estimates determine the policy. Underestimating preventive
efficacy produces repeated action. The reduced model below isolates the question
left open by static point estimates: what can an otherwise accurate learner infer
about efficacy from the outcomes generated by its own policy? Code for a separate
qualitative implementation check of the earlier model remains in the public
repository, but it is not evidence for the present theorem or simulations.

\subsection{Episodes, and the composite the agent can see}

The identifiability analysis uses a reduced description of one
symptom-provoking episode that captures the essential structure of the Rigoux
scenario:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item the episode begins in the hazardous state with probability $q$ (in the
      original, $p_{\mathrm{SOILING}}$);
\item the agent performs $k$ preventive repetitions, each removing the hazard
      with probability $p$ (in the original, $p_{\mathrm{SUCCESS}}$);
\item the agent commits to the consequential action; harm occurs if and only if
      the hazard is still present.
\end{enumerate}
Writing $Y=1$ for harm,
\begin{equation}
Y \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\lamk), \qquad \lamk = q(1-p)^{k}.
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
Everything the agent can observe about $(q,p)$ from outcomes passes through the
scalar $\lamk$. That is the whole of the problem.

\subsection{The Bayesian agent}

The extension replaces the original's static point estimates with a joint posterior over
$(q,p)$ on a $101\times101$ midpoint grid, updated exactly. Priors are
independent Beta distributions; grid inference rather than MCMC keeps every
simulation deterministic given a seed. The agent's subjective expected cost of
committing to $k$ repetitions is
\begin{equation}
C(k) \;=\; c_{\mathrm{harm}}\Big(\mathbb{E}[\lamk] + \kappa\,\mathrm{SD}[\lamk]\Big)
        \;+\; c_{\mathrm{wash}}\,k ,
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
and it selects $k$ by a softmax on $U(k) = -C(k) + \tau\,\mathrm{EIG}(k)$, where
$\mathrm{EIG}(k) = I\big((q,p);Y \mid k, D\big)$ is the expected information gain
of \citet{lindley1956measure} and \citet{chaloner1995bayesian}. The two added
parameters have explicit interpretations: $\kappa>0$ is
intolerance of uncertainty --- the agent pays for the \emph{variance} of the
hazard, not only its mean --- and $\tau>0$ is a willingness to pay for causal
knowledge. The baseline agent has $\kappa=\tau=0$ and is a pure harm minimiser.

Two kinds of uncertainty are distinguished throughout. \emph{State} uncertainty is
the entropy of the agent's belief that the hazard is present in the current
episode at the moment of commitment. \emph{Structural} uncertainty is the entropy
of the posterior over $(q,p)$. The intervention analysis separates observations
of the current state from contrasts that reveal the latent causes, as detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:interventions}.

Knowledge is scored by the log posterior density at the true parameter,
$\log P(q^\ast,p^\ast \mid D)$, not by entropy alone. Entropy rewards confidence,
including the confidently wrong posterior produced by an untreated agent.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Fixed action counts do not identify hazard and efficacy}
\label{sec:theorem}

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-$k$ designs are singular]
\label{prop:rank1}
Let $\theta=(q,p)\in(0,1)^2$ and let $Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\lamk)$ with $\lamk$
as in \eqref{eq:lambda} for a fixed $k\ge 0$. Then the Fisher information of one
observation,
\begin{equation}
\Ifisher_k(\theta) \;=\;
\frac{\nabla_\theta \lamk \, \nabla_\theta \lamk^{\top}}{\lamk(1-\lamk)},
\qquad
\nabla_\theta \lamk =
\big((1-p)^{k},\,-kq(1-p)^{k-1}\big)^{\top},
\label{eq:fisher}
\end{equation}
has rank one and $\det \Ifisher_k(\theta) = 0$ identically. Consequently $(q,p)$
is not locally identifiable from any number of episodes run at a single $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\Ifisher_k$ is a scalar multiple of the outer product of a single vector with
itself, hence has rank at most one; it is exactly one at interior $\theta$ since
$\nabla_\theta\lamk \neq 0$. A $2\times2$ matrix of rank one has zero
determinant. Independent observations add information, so the total information
from $n$ episodes at the same $k$ is $n\Ifisher_k$, still singular. The explicit
ridge in Corollary~\ref{cor:ridge} establishes the failure of local
identifiability directly; the Fisher calculation records its local rank
deficiency. This is the distinction between a parameterisation that is not
identified and an estimator that is merely imprecise.
\end{proof}

The derivation is verified symbolically in \texttt{phase1\_nonidentifiability.py}
and machine-checked in the test suite for a range of parameter values.

\begin{corollary}[The trap has a direction]
\label{cor:ridge}
The null direction of $\Ifisher_k$ is tangent to the iso-likelihood curve
$q = \lambda_0 (1-p)^{-k}$, whose slope is
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq}{dp}\bigg|_{\lamk\ \mathrm{const}} = \frac{kq}{1-p} \;\ge\; 0 .
\label{eq:slope}
\end{equation}
The residual dependence between $q$ and $p$ is therefore \emph{positive}, and the
ridge steepens with $k$: an agent that repeats more cannot tell a dangerous world
it controls from a safe world it does not.
\end{corollary}

Corollary~\ref{cor:ridge} describes the likelihood. Posterior correlation also
depends on how much information the outcomes contain. As $k$ grows,
$\lamk \to 0$ and informative outcomes disappear, so posterior dependence can
fall back towards zero. Fixed policies therefore create two distinct failures:
at moderate $k$ the learner estimates a combination it cannot decompose; at large
$k$ it learns little at all.

\begin{proposition}[Two conditions suffice, and one does not]
\label{prop:two}
For $k_1 \neq k_2$ with $\lambda_{k_1},\lambda_{k_2}$ known,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda_{k_1}}{\lambda_{k_2}} = (1-p)^{k_1-k_2}
\;\Longrightarrow\;
p = 1 - \left(\frac{\lambda_{k_1}}{\lambda_{k_2}}\right)^{1/(k_1-k_2)},
\quad
q = \lambda_{k_1}(1-p)^{-k_1},
\label{eq:recovery}
\end{equation}
so a policy with support on two distinct values of $k$ point-identifies
$(q,p)$. In particular, at $k=0$ the likelihood \eqref{eq:lambda} does not depend
on $p$; response prevention alone identifies $q$ and carries no information about
$p$.
\end{proposition}

For a policy $\pi$ over repetition counts, information adds as
$\Ifisher_\pi = n\sum_k \pi(k)\,\Ifisher_k$. The support of $\pi$, not its mean,
determines rank. In control-theoretic terms, a fixed policy fails to persistently
excite the system: it can reduce immediate risk while remaining uninformative
about the system's causal parameters \citep{bellman1970structural}.

Figure~\ref{fig:fig2} makes Proposition~\ref{prop:rank1} visible. At fixed $k$,
the log-likelihood surface over $(q,p)$ is a ridge rather than a peak (panel A),
and by Corollary~\ref{cor:ridge} the ridge steepens with $k$ (panel B), so
$\det\Ifisher_k$ vanishes for every $k$ (panel C). Comparing candidate policies
at matched episode counts (panel D), all $\nSingularPolicies$ single-$k$ policies
are singular, while every policy with support on two or more values of $k$ is
not. The ordering among the non-singular policies is instructive: the best design
is not the most extreme but the most balanced. A policy alternating $k\in\{0,1\}$
attains $\log_{10}\det\Ifisher_\pi = \detBestTwoPoint$; spreading uniformly over
$k\in\{0,\dots,6\}$ attains only $\detGraded$, because episodes at large $k$
almost never produce an informative outcome. A ``compulsive but jittered'' policy
on $k\in\{5,6,7\}$ is formally non-singular yet practically useless
($\detJittered$; standard error on $p$ of $\seJittered$ versus $\seBestTwoPoint$
for $k\in\{0,1\}$). Variation matters, but variation \emph{where outcomes still
happen}.

\subsection{A repetitive learner predicts outcomes without learning their cause}

Figure~\ref{fig:fig3} follows exact Bayesian agents for $\bayesEpisodes$
episodes in a world with $q^\ast=\worldQ$ and $p^\ast=\worldP$. An agent with a
severe initial doubt about its efficacy that holds its repetition count fixed
ends at $\mathbb{E}[p\mid D] = \bayesFixMeanP$ and
$\mathbb{E}[q\mid D] = \bayesFixMeanQ$, with a posterior correlation of
$\bayesFixCorr$ between the two, and it experiences harm on $\bayesFixHarm\%$ of
episodes. An otherwise identical agent whose policy draws $k$ from
$\{0,1,2,3\}$ ends at $\mathbb{E}[p\mid D] = \bayesVarMeanP$ and
$\mathbb{E}[q\mid D] = \bayesVarMeanQ$ --- both close to the truth --- at the cost
of harm on $\bayesVarHarm\%$ of episodes.

The signature of the trap is the contrast between what is learned and what is
not. The repetitive agent gains $\bayesFixdHlam$ nats about the observable
composite $\lamk$ and $\bayesFixdHp$ nats about $p$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}C). The
same contrast in scale-free terms is starker still: relative to its prior, the
agent's posterior standard deviation for the observable
$\lambda_{\bayesFixKref}$ shrinks by a factor of $\bayesFixSdLamRatio$, while its
uncertainty about $p$ does not shrink at all --- it \emph{grows}, by a factor of
$1/\bayesFixSdPRatio$, as the data push it off a prior that was narrow and wrong
onto a ridge that is broad. It can predict what will happen to it with great
precision while becoming \emph{less} sure why. The diversified agent shows the
opposite pattern: $\bayesVarSdLamRatio\times$ on the observable and
$\bayesVarSdPRatio\times$ on the parameter. (The ratios accompany the
entropies because the entropy of $\lamk$ is computed on a fixed $[0,1]$ bin grid
and saturates when $\lamk$ becomes very small, so the nats figure is a lower
bound on the information gained.) The repetitive agent's final posterior lies
along the analytic ridge (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}D); the diversified agent's sits at
the intersection of the ridges belonging to the different $k$ it used
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}E).

Holding the prior fixed and varying only the design
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}F) shows that information about one's own efficacy is
maximised at an intermediate and rather small number of repetitions
($k=\sweepBestK$; $\sweepBestdHp$ nats) and falls away at both ends: exactly zero
at $k=0$, where the likelihood does not depend on $p$, and $\sweepTaildHp$ nats
at $k=\sweepKmax$, where harm has become so rare that no outcome is surprising.
Posterior coupling between $q$ and $p$ behaves the same way, peaking at
$\sweepMaxCorr$ and vanishing at $k=0$ ($\sweepCorrAtZero$). The two ends of the
range are two different failures: at $k=0$ the agent learns a parameter that is
not the one it cares about; at large $k$ it learns nothing at all.

\subsection{Belief error persists without persistent repetition}

A purely harm-minimising agent that begins in doubt does not continue repeating
at the same rate. Its mean action count falls from $\kappaZeroEarlyK$ early to
$\kappaZeroLateK$ late, even though its efficacy belief remains far from the
truth ($|\mathbb{E}[p\mid D]-p^\ast| = \kappaZeroErrP$). The epistemic failure
therefore survives partial behavioural normalisation.

The model represents intolerance of uncertainty as $\kappa$ in
\eqref{eq:cost}: the agent pays for the variance of the hazard as well as its
mean. Increasing $\kappa$ raises mean safety behaviour from $\kappaZeroK$ to
$\kappaMaxK$ ($r = \kappaCorrK$) and increases residual efficacy error from
$\kappaZeroErrP$ to $\kappaMaxErrP$ ($r = \kappaCorrErr$). It does not prevent
behavioural decline over $\bayesEpisodes$ episodes. Even at
$\kappa=\kappaMax$, the action count falls from $\kappaMaxEarlyK$ early to
$\kappaMaxLateK$ late.

The result sets a firm boundary. Uncertainty aversion produces more repetition
and worse knowledge, but the simulated behaviour is not self-sustaining. The
model explains why causal beliefs can remain wrong; it does not, by itself,
explain why repetitive behaviour persists. A model of chronic persistence needs
at least one additional mechanism tested independently.

\subsection{Informative contrasts restore causal knowledge}
\label{sec:interventions}

The simulation compares eight arms. Figure~\ref{fig:fig6} displays the six
intervention arms that share the same repetitive pre-treatment history and are
therefore directly comparable. Continued free choice serves as the untreated
reference in the reported contrasts, while response prevention from a naive start
is analysed separately because it lacks that history. All $\nHypTotal$
pre-specified contrasts are listed in
\texttt{results/phase3\_hypotheses.csv}.

\paragraph{Response prevention raises expected risk.} During treatment, the
agent's probability that harm is imminent at commitment rises to
$\erpRiskDuring$ under response prevention versus $\freeRiskDuring$ under
continued free choice. This quantity is expected risk, not experienced distress;
the reduced model contains no affective process.

\paragraph{Response prevention is not a probe of efficacy.} With no repetitive
history to contrast against, response prevention moves the agent's efficacy
belief by $\erpNaiveShiftP$ --- exactly nothing, as
Proposition~\ref{prop:two} requires --- while the same intervention applied after
a repetitive block moves it by $\erpAfterShiftP$. Earlier repetitive episodes
pin down the composite $\lambda_{k}$, response-prevention
episodes pin down $q$, and the two together determine $p$. Response prevention
works as the second half of a contrast and is inert as the first half.

\paragraph{It is diversification that does the work.} A control condition that
holds $k$ at its pre-treatment value --- the same number of preventive actions,
no variation --- gains $\lockedGain$ nats of knowledge about the truth, against
$\bestDiversifiedGain$ nats for the best diversified condition. Meanwhile the
untreated agent becomes \emph{more} confident and no more correct: at follow-up
its posterior places the true baseline hazard $\zqFree$ posterior standard
deviations away, against $\zqVariable$ for the diversified agent.

\paragraph{Probe timing separates state and causal information.} A state probe
performed after the preventive action removes $\reassuranceState$ nats of
current-state uncertainty while adding only $\reassuranceGain$ nats of structural
knowledge, against $\variableGain$ for the diversified policy
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig6}D). Moving the same probe before the action yields
$\baselineCheckGain$ nats. Its cost and accuracy are unchanged; only its position
in the causal sequence differs. A post-action observation can establish that the
current outcome is safe without showing whether the action produced that safety.

\paragraph{The trap has no fixed behavioural direction.} By the end of the pre-treatment block every agent has settled at
$k \approx \preMeanK$ with $\mathbb{E}[p\mid D] = \preMeanP$ --- still far below
the true $\worldP$ --- and, more strikingly, $\mathbb{E}[q\mid D] = \preMeanQ$
against a true baseline hazard of $\worldQ$, a value its own posterior places
$\preZq$ standard deviations away. The trapped agent has resolved its
non-identifiability in the wrong direction: it has concluded that the world is
safe rather than that its actions work. Every condition that supplies a genuine
probe of $q$ corrects this ($\mathbb{E}[q\mid D] = \postVarQ$ under diversified
exposure, against $\postFreeQ$ if untreated) and consequently \emph{raises} the
follow-up repetition rate, to $\postKtreated$ against $\postKuntreated$ for the
untreated agent, with pure response prevention highest at $\postErpK$.

This result is not a prediction that treatment makes people perform more safety
actions. It exposes
a boundary of the reduced model: rank deficiency can support ``the world is
dangerous and my action works'' or ``the world is safe and my action does not.''
The reliable prediction is a confidently wrong causal model, not a fixed direction
of behavioural change.

\subsection{Initial doubt determines where learning fails}

Sweeping the agent's initial belief about its own efficacy against the cost of
harm (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}) locates the trap. Initial doubt dominates: residual
error in the efficacy belief
falls from $\phaseHighDoubtErr$ for the most doubtful agents to
$\phaseLowDoubtErr$ for the most confident ($r = \phaseCorrDoubtErr$). The cost of
harm sets how much safety behaviour is performed ($r = \phaseCorrCostK$ against
$\log$ cost) but has no monotone relation to residual error once the agent acts
at all ($r = \phaseCorrCostErrHigh$ for costs above $6$).

What organises the whole diagram is the realised repetition count, and it does so
in the shape of a U (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig4}D). Agents that settle at $k=0$ --- those
for whom harm is cheap enough not to bother --- end $\phaseErrAtZeroK$ away from
the truth about their own efficacy, because $k=0$ is uninformative about $p$ by
Proposition~\ref{prop:two}; they pay for this with a harm rate of
$\phaseHarmAtZeroK\%$ and learn nothing from it. Agents that settle at
$k \approx \phaseBestK$ end $\phaseBestErr$ away, at a harm rate of
$\phaseHarmAtBestK\%$. Agents driven to $k \approx \phaseWorstHighK$ by a high
cost of harm end $\phaseErrHighK$ away, having experienced essentially no harm at
all. This is the same interior optimum found in the controlled design sweep
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig3}F), now emerging from agents that were never told what to do:
never acting and always acting are both blind, and the cost of harm decides which
kind of blindness you get.

The information-seeking weight is the escape axis, and what it changes is
instructive. Among the most doubtful third of agents, residual error falls from
$\tauLoErrZero$ at $\tau=0$ to $\tauLoErrMax$ at $\tau=\tauMax$, and the efficacy
belief moves from $\tauLoMeanPZero$ to $\tauLoMeanPMax$ against a true $\worldP$.
What it changes to achieve this is worth being precise about, because it is not
what one might guess. The agent does not act much less
($\tauLoKZero$ repetitions against $\tauLoKMax$), and it does not visibly vary
more (variance of the action count $\tauLoVarZero$ against $\tauLoVarMax$). What
rises is the harm it actually experiences, from $\tauLoHarmZero\%$ of episodes to
$\tauLoHarmMax\%$. The escape is a shift towards repetition counts at which
outcomes still \emph{happen}, which is the same lesson the policy comparison in
Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}D teaches: a ``compulsive but jittered'' policy on
$k \in \{5,6,7\}$ is formally non-singular and practically useless. Variation
buys nothing where nothing ever occurs.

Two caveats. The effect requires a substantial $\tau$: below about
$\tau \approx 45$ nothing moves, because the epistemic bonus is small next to the
cost of harm. And, as the hysteresis simulations make visible, the expected
information gain of an episode can be maximised at a \emph{large} $k$ when the
agent is pessimistic, so an information-seeking agent does not always explore
downward.

\subsection{Belief recalibration is path dependent under fixed policies}

Ramping the objective hazard up and back down (Fig.~\ref{fig:fig5}) produces
history dependence in every arm. A loop alone is not diagnostic because any
belief-updating agent lags a moving world; indeed, loop areas do not separate the arms
($\loopLocked$ for the locked design against $\loopQuota$ for an agent with a
standing $25\%$ response-prevention quota). What does separate them is
\emph{calibration}, and it does so asymmetrically. On the rising limb every arm
tracks the hazard about equally well ($|\mathbb{E}[q\mid D]-q| = \calibLocked$ up,
against $\calibQuota$ up). On the falling limb the locked design comes apart:
$\calibLockedDown$ against $\calibQuotaDown$ for the quota agent and
$\calibCheckerDown$ for an agent with a pre-action state probe. The trap is specifically a failure
to notice that the world has become safer, which is what the theory predicts: the
evidence that would carry that news is the evidence a repetitive policy suppresses.

Removing the hazard outright sharpens this into a single number. After the hazard
is set to zero, the locked agent takes $\stepLockedHalve$ episodes to halve its
repetition rate, still performs $\stepLockedK$ repetitions per episode at the end
of a $1500$-episode horizon, and believes the baseline hazard to be
$\stepLockedQ$ in a world where it is exactly zero. An agent with a standing
response-prevention quota takes $\stepQuotaHalve$ episodes and ends at
$\stepQuotaQ$; an information-seeking agent takes $\stepCuriousHalve$ --- five
times faster than the locked agent --- and ends at $\stepCuriousQ$.

The locked agent's terminal beliefs show the ridge directly. It ends convinced both that its actions are highly
effective ($\mathbb{E}[p\mid D] = \stepLockedP$) \emph{and} that the world remains
dangerous ($\stepLockedQ$) --- a pair that reproduces the harm rate it observes
(namely zero) exactly as well as the truth does. Every arm that supplies a
contrast converges instead on the correct low hazard. The result concerns delayed
belief recalibration under constrained policies. It does not show that the model
endogenously generates chronic compulsive behaviour.

\subsection{A matched-exposure experiment can test the account}

A direct causal test should assign the action schedule rather than infer its
effect from naturally varying behaviour. Participants would complete a
computerized latent-hazard task in one of four between-participant conditions:
$k=3$ on every trial, equal mixtures of $k\in\{2,4\}$, equal mixtures of
$k\in\{1,5\}$, or a uniform schedule over $k\in\{0,\ldots,6\}$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig7}A). Every schedule has $\mathbb{E}[k]=3$, so the number of
actions and their total cost are matched. The $\{1,5\}$ and uniform schedules
also have the same raw variance, $\mathrm{Var}(k)=4$, but distribute observations
differently. Their contrast separates the amount of variation from the
information carried by its support.

Participants would observe only the terminal binary outcome. Before learning and
after each block, they would estimate baseline hazard $q$, preventive efficacy
$p$, and their confidence in each estimate. The preregistered primary outcome
would be absolute error in the final efficacy estimate. Secondary outcomes would
be error in $q$, confidence calibration, prediction of the observable composite
$\lambda_k$, and experienced loss. A final free-choice transfer block could test
whether acquired causal knowledge changes subsequent action, but that behavioural
outcome would remain secondary to the learning test.

The simulation yields three ordered predictions. First, the fixed schedule should
produce the least precise and least accurate efficacy estimates. Second, varying
the action count should improve learning even when mean exposure is unchanged.
Third, the uniform schedule should outperform $\{1,5\}$ despite equal means and
variances, because it samples more informative outcomes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fig7}B,C). Across conditions, expected Fisher information or
expected information gain should predict efficacy accuracy better than raw
$\mathrm{Var}(k)$.

The true values of $(q,p)$, number of trials, and sample size should be fixed only
after simulation-based parameter-recovery and power analyses. In particular, a
regime that makes harm almost absent under $k=3$ would provide too few events for
a practical human test even though it illustrates the theorem. The account would
be challenged if an adequately powered and recoverable experiment found no
advantage of informative variation over the fixed schedule, or if schedules with
equal raw variance produced indistinguishable learning despite different expected
information.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the model establishes}

The central result is structural. A fixed action count reveals the composite
$q(1-p)^k$ but cannot identify baseline hazard and preventive efficacy
separately. An exact Bayesian learner cannot recover information that its policy
does not generate. Variation across action counts restores identification, but
only when it samples outcomes that remain informative. These claims do not
require a biased prior, a faulty update rule, or a habit system.

The simulations establish a second, equally important result. Causal error can
persist after the action rate begins to decline. The information failure is
therefore neither equivalent to behavioural severity nor sufficient to explain
chronic repetition. Table~\ref{tab:accounts} places this narrower account beside
neighbouring proposals rather than treating it as their replacement.

\subsection{Implications for intervention research}

Response prevention can be viewed as an experimental contrast as well as a
clinical procedure. Inhibitory learning theory explains exposure through
expectancy violation \citep{craske2014inhibitory}. The present analysis adds a
more specific prediction: $k=0$ identifies baseline hazard but not preventive
efficacy. After a repetitive history, it supplies a second action count and can
therefore help identify both parameters. From a naive start it does not identify
efficacy by itself.

This is a prediction about causal learning, not treatment effectiveness. The
model contains no distress dynamics, therapeutic alliance, inhibitory memory, or
clinical outcome. It therefore does not show that one exposure protocol reduces
symptoms better than another. It predicts only that protocols creating
informative contrasts across action counts should produce more accurate knowledge
of efficacy, a claim that can be tested with the matched-exposure experiment.

A state probe illustrates a related temporal distinction. A probe after the
preventive action can resolve whether the current state is safe while remaining
uninformative about whether the action caused that safety. Moving the same probe
before the action can reveal baseline hazard. This manipulation is experimentally
testable, but its correspondence to reassurance in clinical practice remains an
open question.

\subsection{Knowledge of control requires informative alternatives}

The mathematics expresses a simple constraint on self-knowledge. Observing good
outcomes after acting does not establish control; the action must vary and its
alternatives must remain observable. Research on the sense of agency has largely
studied the subjective experience of control
\citep{haggard2017sense,moore2016agency,gentsch2012agency};
the present account concerns knowledge of controllability and the conditions
under which it can be learned. It is a special case of a general point in causal learning:
identifying a causal structure requires choosing informative interventions, not
merely observing outcomes \citep{he2008active}. What is distinctive about the
clinical case is that the agent choosing the interventions is the same agent whose
own efficacy is the parameter in question.

\subsection{The identification problem is general}

The mathematical structure is not specific to compulsion. A controller that
regulates a system too consistently may cease to excite it and lose the ability
to identify it. Self-confirming
equilibria preserve beliefs about contingencies that are never visited
\citep{fudenberg1993selfconfirming}; selective labels and algorithmic feedback
loops arise when earlier decisions determine which outcomes later become visible
\citep{lakkaraju2017selective,chaney2018algorithmic}. Safe reinforcement learning
faces the same tension between exploration and protection
\citep{moldovan2012safe}. The common principle is that a policy controls both
outcomes and the evidence available for learning their causes. These parallels
motivate generalisation, but they are analogies rather than empirical results of
the present model.

\subsection{Limitations}

The clearest limitation is also the clearest boundary on the claim. The model
explains why causal beliefs can remain wrong; it does not, by itself, explain why
repetitive behaviour persists. A purely harm-minimising agent reduces its action
count even while its beliefs remain inaccurate, and uncertainty aversion does not
close that behavioural loop. Chronic repetition requires at least one additional
mechanism, such as habit, affective relief, memory processes, or changing costs,
specified and tested independently.

The hazard model is a deliberate reduction. The agent commits to $k$ before the
episode rather than choosing action by action. The environment is stationary
except in the path-dependence analysis, and change is handled by exponential
forgetting rather than a change-point model. Harm is observed without noise;
adding observation noise would not restore identification and would generally
reduce information. The intervention simulations isolate action-count variation,
whereas real exposure and response prevention changes several processes at once.

The model applies most directly to preventive actions that alter a latent hazard,
such as washing when contamination and its removal cannot be inspected directly.
This is not the same as observing a state. As \citet{prudon2021} emphasises, door
or stove checking can provide precise sensory evidence and may depend instead on
memory, metacognition, responsibility, or the meaning of the routine. Treating
all checking as repeated prevention would exceed the model's scope. The
post-action probe in the simulations is an observation operator, not a complete
model of compulsive checking or reassurance.

Finally, no human data are analysed. The proposed matched-exposure experiment
requires parameter-recovery, model-confusion, and power analyses before data
collection. Until that test is run, the link from policy-induced
non-identifiability to human causal beliefs remains a prediction.
